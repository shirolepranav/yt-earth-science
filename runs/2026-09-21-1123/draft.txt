August, two thousand fourteen. On the eastern lobe of the southern Aral Sea, there is no water at all. Not a shallow pool, not a muddy remnant — dry ground, cracked into pale plates, running out to the horizon in the desert heat of Central Asia. According to NASA's Earth Observatory, that basin went completely dry that August.

Fifty-four years earlier, in nineteen sixty, that same body of water was part of the fourth-largest inland water body on Earth. According to NASA's Earth Observatory, it has since lost ninety percent of its volume and shrunk to roughly a tenth of its former size. You are standing on a seabed you could once have sailed.

The Aral isn't a sea. It's a lake with no outlet, fed by two rivers, the Amu Darya from the south and the Syr Darya from the north. Aladin and colleagues date its origin to roughly seventeen thousand years ago. Water arrives, and water leaves. But it leaves only as vapour. That single fact is the hinge this whole story turns on.

So what happened to the rivers?

In the nineteen fifties, Soviet planners looked at those two rivers and saw something other than a sea. They saw cotton — "white gold" — and a desert they intended to farm. So they took the rivers.

The Kara Kum Canal opened in nineteen fifty-six. According to Guy Fipps of Texas A&M University, that one canal diverted a third of the Amu Darya's flow in the year it opened, into newly irrigated land in Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. Not a third of a trickle. A third of the river that fed the sea.

And the diversion kept growing. Fipps records that roughly twenty dams and reservoirs and sixty major irrigation schemes went up across the basin over the following decades. Central Asian irrigation experts quoted in his work estimate that only twenty to twenty-five percent of the water pulled from those rivers ever reached the crops. The rest leaked from unlined canals or evaporated on the way.

So the sea's income was cut. Its outgoings weren't. The desert kept drawing water out of it at exactly the rate it always had. What happens to a basin when fewer drops come in, and every drop that leaves it leaves as vapour?

Stand on the shore in the nineteen sixties and you'd see nothing change from one year to the next. It was the kind of shift a fisherman measured by where his boat sat against the dock. But by nineteen seventy, the level had fallen past the range of natural fluctuation the sea had shown for the previous ten thousand years.

Here's how we know: hydrologists worked out the water budget term by term — river inflow, rainfall, groundwater, evaporation. Columbia University's Aral Sea Crisis project, drawing on Micklin's two thousand seven analysis, gives the result. In the first decade of desiccation, the water level fell by about twenty-one centimetres a year. In the next decade, it fell by about fifty-seven. Then it accelerated further.

Why would a shrinking sea shrink faster? Because it got shallower. The same sunlight now had to heat a smaller volume of water, so the surface warmed faster, and warmer water evaporates faster, and the level dropped further still. The Aral was paying for its own disappearance out of a shrinking account.

Between nineteen sixty and nineteen ninety, according to Guy Fipps of Texas A&M, the sea lost about six hundred cubic kilometres of water. Picture a cube of water eight kilometres along every edge. Now picture it gone. And the water that left took nothing with it, which raises the question of what it left behind.

Picture the shore of a fishing town in the late nineteen seventies. The boats are still tied up, or run aground, or sitting upright on sand. Photographs from the region show exactly that — trawlers stranded kilometres from open water. They stopped sailing because the water left. But there's a second reason they stayed.

The salt stayed. Every dissolved mineral the rivers carried in stayed in the basin when the water evaporated off. Salinity rose from about ten parts per thousand in nineteen sixty to ninety-two parts per thousand by two thousand four, according to Zavialov's measurements cited in Columbia University's Aral Sea Crisis project. The University of West Florida's environmental science text puts the southern sea at that point at roughly three times the salt of seawater.

Fish can't live in that. According to Bosch, cited in the same Columbia project, the commercial fishery that landed forty-three thousand four hundred and thirty tons in nineteen sixty landed nothing at all by nineteen eighty. Twenty years, from a working industry to zero. The University of West Florida's text records twenty-four species of fish in that water. They could not adapt to the rising salt, and they are gone.

And the salt didn't stay in the basin either. NASA's Earth Observatory describes winds lifting salt-laden sediment off the exposed seabed and dropping tens of thousands of tons of it a year onto farmland and towns downwind. Fipps notes that dust is likely to carry pesticide and chemical residues, and links it to serious health problems across the region.

The sea had been doing one other job, quietly. It moderated the climate of a desert. The University of West Florida's text notes that the regional climate is now drier, with greater temperature extremes. The lake was weather. So what happens when a body of water that size tears itself apart?

In the late nineteen eighties, satellites passing over Central Asia began sending back images of something no map had ever shown. The Aral was no longer one body of water. It had torn in two.

According to Aladin and colleagues, writing in the journal Zoological Studies in two thousand twenty-three, the surface area fell from sixty-seven thousand four hundred and ninety-nine square kilometres in nineteen sixty to thirty-nine thousand seven hundred and thirty-four by nineteen ninety. Nearly twenty-eight thousand square kilometres of water surface, gone in thirty years.

And the water didn't retreat to a tidy new shoreline. According to the University of West Florida's text, the present shore sits tens of kilometres from towns that were once ports. The sea didn't just leave. It left a desert between itself and everyone who used to live on it.

The Amu Darya delta is another casualty. Fipps records that the delta once covered five hundred and fifty thousand hectares of wetland and reed bed. It has shrunk to less than twenty thousand.

The shallower the basin, the sooner a falling level cuts it into pieces. The northern Small Aral separated from the southern Large Aral, each now fed by a different river. The Large Aral split again, into an eastern lobe and a western one. Every fragment got its own depth, its own salt, its own odds.

In two thousand five, Kazakhstan finished the Kokaral Dam, sealing off the northern sea to hold its water in. NASA's Earth Observatory reports that fisheries in the north rebounded. The same agency's earlier reporting is blunter: the dam ended any prospect of recovery for the southern sea, which some authorities already regarded as beyond help.

So one half of the Aral is recovering and one half is not. Why would the southern half fail that completely, and how far does the failure go?

Hypersalinity doesn't stop at the point where the fish die. Aladin and colleagues, writing in Zoological Studies in two thousand twenty-three, describe the remnants of the southern Large Aral as progressing toward what they call a "Dead Sea" condition, water hostile to all animal life. Not depleted. Not degraded. Finished, for anything that swims or crawls.

And it isn't a smooth decline. NASA's Earth Observatory records that the eastern lobe was completely dry in August two thousand fourteen. In twenty seventeen, a wet year, it partly refilled, helped by large releases from the Toktogul reservoir and an unusually warm spring that melted snow and glacial ice in the Tien Shan.

But Philip Micklin, the geographer who has studied this sea for decades, told NASA those events do not signal a restoration of the eastern lobe as a permanent feature. What he expects is the pattern itself — refilling in heavy flow years, near-total dry-out in low-flow years, for the foreseeable future.

That's where the science is still open: whether that oscillation is the new steady state, or just the last stage of a one-way decline with the wet years as interruptions. The evidence currently favours Micklin's reading. What nobody disputes is the arithmetic that produced it.

The Aral had no outlet. Every drop that left it left as vapour, which means every drop of river water taken upstream was a drop subtracted from the sea permanently. Not borrowed. Not delayed. Subtracted.

The Great Salt Lake has no outlet either. According to the US Geological Survey, consumptive water use has reduced net river inflow to that lake by thirty-nine percent over the past one hundred and fifty years. The Aral isn't a story about a faraway place. It's the same sum, running in a different desert, and that one, you can drive to.

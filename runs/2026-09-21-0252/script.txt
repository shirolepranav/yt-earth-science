Here is a fact that sounds backwards: the ice sheets of the Northern Hemisphere were not destroyed by a catastrophe. They were destroyed by a slightly warmer summer. According to a 2013 study in the journal Nature, once the North American ice sheet had grown large enough, a moderate increase in summer sunlight pushed its mass balance negative, and it retreated almost completely within a few thousand years. The bigger the ice got, the readier it was to fail.

That pattern has been running for two and a half million years, according to UC Santa Barbara. The growth was slow. The endings were fast.

That lopsided rhythm, long build and sudden failure, is written into sea level, into the shape of the continents, into where you are allowed to live. You are standing in one of the warm intervals right now. The thing driving the whole pattern is a clock nobody has finished reading. So let's take it apart, in order, and see where the knowing stops.

Earth does not spin upright. Its axis leans, and the lean changes. According to NASA Science, over the last million years that tilt has swung between twenty-two point one degrees and twenty-four point five degrees and back again, on a cycle that takes about forty-one thousand years. Today the axis sits at twenty-three point four degrees, roughly halfway between the extremes, and it is very slowly decreasing. NASA notes that it was last at maximum tilt about ten thousand years ago, and will reach its minimum about ten thousand years from now.

Why does a lean matter? Because it changes where the sunlight lands, not how much the planet gets. A larger tilt means each hemisphere leans further into the Sun in summer and further away in winter, so the seasons sharpen, and the effect is strongest at high northern latitudes. Those are the latitudes where ice sheets live or die. NASA states it plainly: larger tilt angles favour periods of deglaciation.

Two point four degrees of total travel, spread across forty-one thousand years. In a single human lifetime, the axis moves so little you would need an instrument to notice. And it helps decide whether ice sits on top of Canada.

But the tilt has been swinging for millions of years. If it were the whole story, the ice ages would beat like a metronome. Warm, cold, warm, cold, every forty-one thousand years. They do not. So what does the wobble do?

Stand outside on the third of January and you are closer to the Sun than at any other moment of the year, about five point one million kilometres closer, according to NASA. You are also freezing. That is precession, and it is the reason the seasons and the orbit do not line up the way you would assume.

It comes in two numbers, and people mix them up. NASA Science puts the wobble of the axis against the fixed stars, axial precession, at twenty-five thousand seven hundred seventy-one point five years. But the seasons cycle against the orbit faster, in roughly twenty-one thousand years, because the ellipse itself is rotating as the axis turns. A Milankovitch cycles reference hosted by Harvard's school of engineering gives the same picture. About twenty-six thousand years for the full turn of the axis. About twenty-one thousand years for the beat between the seasons and the orbit. That distinction matters the moment you try to match the cycle to the rock record.

Watch what it does. NASA says perihelion, our closest approach, currently falls around the third of January, and aphelion, the farthest point, around the fourth of July. That means each January, about six point eight percent more incoming solar radiation reaches Earth than it does each July. When the orbit is at its most elliptical, that gap widens to about twenty-three percent. Precession does not change the total sunlight. It changes the timing and the intensity of the seasons, and it decides whether the northern summer peak lands near the Sun or far from it.

Now you have two real cycles, forty-one thousand years and twenty-one thousand years, both measurable in the rock. Neither of them is one hundred thousand years. So where does the one hundred thousand year beat come from?

The problem has a date. During a stretch called the mid-Pleistocene transition, roughly one point two five million to seven hundred thousand years ago according to a 2023 study in Nature Geoscience, the rhythm of the ice ages changed. Before it, the cycles were dominated by forty-one thousand years. After it, strong one hundred thousand year power appears in the climate record. And according to a study in Nature, that switch happened without any apparent change in the insolation forcing.

Of the three classic Milankovitch parameters, only eccentricity has variations anywhere near one hundred thousand years, and even there the reference gives a set of terms between ninety-five thousand and one hundred thirty-six thousand years that only loosely combine into the beat. The Milankovitch reference gives the range as an eccentricity of zero point zero zero five to zero point zero five eight, with a mean of zero point zero two eight. Today it sits at zero point zero one seven.

And there is the catch that has annoyed researchers for fifty years. Eccentricity barely changes the total energy Earth receives. NASA Science says the change in global annual insolation from the eccentricity cycle is very small, and calls eccentricity a relatively minor factor in seasonal climate. So the loudest rhythm in the ice record appears to be paced by the quietest knob in the orbital system. How does a quiet knob write a loud beat?

A piston corer comes up over the rail of a research ship in the North Atlantic. Inside the tube is a column of grey mud, layer on layer of microscopic shells that sank to the seafloor over hundreds of thousands of years. Each layer records how much of the planet's water was locked in ice at that moment.

Here's how we know: in 2025, a team led by Stephen Barker at Cardiff University published an analysis in the journal Science. They took a million year record of climate change, changes in the size of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets and in deep ocean temperature, and matched it against the shape of Earth's orbit, its wobble, and its tilt. Then they did something different from everyone before them. Instead of trying to date individual events precisely, they looked at the shape of the climate record and asked how the orbital parameters fit together.

According to UC Santa Barbara's report on the work, the team found a clear imprint of the different orbital parameters on the climate record. Every glaciation of the past nine hundred thousand years, the study reports, follows the same sequence. Deglaciation begins with the first precession peak that starts while obliquity is rising, and that peak arrives immediately after a minimum in eccentricity. Glacial inception then follows with the next decrease in obliquity. One type of orbital change ends the ice ages. Another brings them back.

Notice what that does. Eccentricity never has to force the climate directly. It only has to sit at its minimum, damping the amplitude of precession, and let whichever precession peaks survive become the ones that can end an ice age. On this reading, the one hundred thousand year beat is a grouping rather than a pulse, a counting out of precession cycles, gated by eccentricity.

Barker's team called the pattern largely deterministic, and used it to predict when each interglacial of the past million years would occur, and how long it would last. But that is one strong result. It is not a settled field. The one hundred thousand year beat has a habit of outrunning every explanation we hand it.

A 2025 paper in Nature Communications traces the same cycles to internal carbon, a coupling between ice volume and the carbon cycle that recurs on a two point four million year eccentricity rhythm, and that the authors argue amplifies the one hundred thousand year signal. Others back a hypothesis called obliquity skipping, described in that 2023 Nature Geoscience paper, which holds that ice ages simply terminate every two or three of the forty-one thousand year cycles. That averages out to about a hundred thousand.

Then there are the older candidates. In 1997, Richard Muller and Gordon MacDonald proposed in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences that the one hundred thousand year cycle tracks the inclination of Earth's orbital plane rather than eccentricity. Other researchers have since called that an artifact of spectral analysis, so treat it as a live disagreement, not an answer.

Nicholas Shackleton's 2000 paper in Science used oxygen isotopes trapped in Antarctic ice to separate ice volume from deep water temperature. He found that at the one hundred thousand year period, carbon dioxide and temperature sit in phase with orbital eccentricity, while ice volume lags behind all three. That puts the carbon cycle, not the ice, closer to the driver's seat.

And then there is the record that refuses to cooperate. The US Geological Survey studied a calcite core from Devils Hole, a fault-created cave in the Nevada desert. The core is thirty-six centimetres long, about the length of your forearm, and it holds five hundred thousand years of climate, dated by uranium and thorium isotopes. According to the USGS, between three hundred fifty thousand and four hundred fifty thousand years ago, when insolation in both hemispheres barely changed at all, that record still shows a full glacial and interglacial cycle. The USGS also notes that Devils Hole suggests the last four interglacials each lasted around twenty thousand years, twice the commonly assumed length.

According to the USGS, this warm interval has run about ten thousand years, though UC Santa Barbara dates the end of the last glaciation to around eleven thousand seven hundred years ago. So the clock is still running.

The Science team behind the 2025 analysis estimates that, on its own, Earth would shift toward another ice age in roughly ten thousand years. Then they add the part that matters. That transition is very unlikely to arrive on schedule, because the carbon dioxide already in the atmosphere has diverted the climate from its natural course.

Which is the reason the beat is worth knowing at all. Every coastline, every field, every city you have ever stood in was built during the ten thousand years since the ice last let go, in the gap between two beats, on a clock we only partly understand.
